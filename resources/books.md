# Books worth the shelf space

> Twenty-three books that repay close reading. Preference is given to books with durable mental models, not framework tutorials. Free or officially available editions are marked.

## Systems and distributed data

| Book | Why read it | Level |
|---|---|---|
| [Designing Data-Intensive Applications](https://dataintensive.net/) — Martin Kleppmann | The best single map of replication, partitioning, transactions, consistency, and stream processing. | Intermediate |
| [Database Internals](https://www.databass.dev/) — Alex Petrov | How storage engines and distributed databases actually work, from B-trees to consensus. | Advanced |
| [Transaction Processing](https://www.elsevier.com/books/transaction-processing/gray/978-1-55860-190-1) — Gray and Reuter | The classic treatment of atomicity, isolation, recovery, and locking. | Advanced |
| [Site Reliability Engineering](https://sre.google/sre-book/table-of-contents/) — Google | How production is operated, measured, and made resilient. Free online. | Intermediate |
| [The Site Reliability Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/table-of-contents/) — Google | Practical SLOs, incident response, and release engineering. Free online. | Intermediate |
| [Release It!](https://pragprog.com/titles/mnee2/release-it-second-edition/) — Michael Nygard | Circuit breakers, bulkheads, and the failure modes that appear only in production. | Intermediate |
| [Designing Distributed Systems](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/designing-distributed-systems/9781491983638/) — Brendan Burns | Reusable patterns: sidecars, ambassadors, sharding, and ownership. | Intermediate |

## Computer science foundations

| Book | Why read it | Level |
|---|---|---|
| [Introduction to Algorithms](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262046305/introduction-to-algorithms/) — CLRS | The reference for algorithms and complexity; use it as a companion, not a novel. | Intermediate |
| [Computer Systems: A Programmer's Perspective](https://csapp.cs.cmu.edu/) — Bryant and O'Hallaron | Connects code to memory, assembly, caching, and the machine. | Beginner → intermediate |
| [Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~remzi/OSTEP/) — Arpaci-Dusseau | Free, readable coverage of virtualisation, concurrency, and persistence. | Beginner → intermediate |
| [Structure and Interpretation of Computer Programs](https://sarabander.github.io/sicp/) — Abelson and Sussman | Abstraction, recursion, interpreters, and a different way of thinking. Free online. | Intermediate |
| [The Art of Multiprocessor Programming](https://www.elsevier.com/books/the-art-of-multiprocessor-programming/herlihy/978-0-12-415950-1) — Herlihy and Shavit | The theory behind locks, atomics, and concurrent data structures. | Advanced |
| [Types and Programming Languages](https://www.cis.upenn.edu/~bcpierce/tapl/) — Benjamin Pierce | A rigorous introduction to type systems and operational semantics. | Advanced |

| Book | Why read it | Level |
|---|---|---|
| [Computer Networking: A Top-Down Approach](https://gaia.cs.umass.edu/kurose_ross/index.php) — Kurose and Ross | The clearest path from applications down to packets. | Beginner → intermediate |
| [High Performance Browser Networking](https://hpbn.co/) — Ilya Grigorik | HTTP, TLS, TCP, UDP, and the browser's network stack. Free online. | Intermediate |

## How to read a technical book

1. Read the table of contents and the first chapter to build a map.
2. Read for the argument, not every paragraph.
3. Keep a one-page summary of the model and the trade-offs.
4. Apply one idea to a real system within a week.
5. Revisit the hard chapters after you have hit the problem in practice.

> A book you annotate and apply is worth ten you only finish.
